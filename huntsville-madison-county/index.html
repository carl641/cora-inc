Service Area

Inside the Huntsville city limits, most homes tie into public sewer. Drive ten minutes past them and that changes in a hurry — across most of Madison County, wastewater is handled by a tank and a drain field sitting in your own yard. Those systems are what we work on every day.

Madison County Residents

Most of Madison County runs on septic, not sewer

Huntsville and Madison run municipal sewer inside their city limits. Almost everywhere else in the county — Hazel Green, Toney, Harvest, Meridianville, Moores Mill, Big Cove, New Hope, Gurley, Owens Cross Roads, Brownsboro, Maysville — the house has its own septic tank and its own field lines. There is no sewer bill, and there is no utility crew on call. The system is part of the property, and keeping it working falls to whoever owns it.

That is who we work for. Most of our days are spent on county roads and long gravel driveways: pumping tanks for families who have not had a truck out in a decade, finding lids that got buried when a deck or a flower bed went in, and catching the problems that turn into a backup at the worst possible moment.

  • Routine pump-outs every 3–5 years, sized to your household and tank
  • Tank locating for county properties with no records and no visible lid
  • Drain field diagnosis for soggy yards, standing water, and slow drains
  • Risers and lids installed so the next service call does not mean digging
  • Documented inspections for county home sales and refinancing
  • 24/7 emergency response when a tank backs up into the house

Local Conditions

What Madison County ground does to a septic system

Madison County is not one kind of ground, and septic systems feel the difference. Up on Monte Sano, Green Mountain, Keel Mountain, and Chapman Mountain, there is often a thin layer of soil over limestone rock — a drain field up there has less room to work with and far less margin when a household overloads it. Down in the flats along the Flint River and the Tennessee, the water table sits high and field lines stay saturated long after a wet spring. Out through Harvest, Monrovia, and Toney, subdivisions have gone in fast on land that used to be farmed, which puts a lot of newer systems on smaller lots where placement matters more than most homeowners realize.

Older county homesteads are their own story. Plenty of Madison County property is running on tanks set decades ago — heavy concrete lids, no risers, baffles that have rotted out, field lines laid before anyone thought to map them. We open those up, look at what is actually in there, and tell you plainly whether the system is fine, tired, or close to failing.

  • Thin soil over rock on the mountain properties
  • High water tables near the Flint and Tennessee Rivers
  • Decades-old tanks with failed baffles, buried lids, and no risers
  • New construction on smaller lots through Harvest, Monrovia, and Toney
  • Onsite sewage permits handled through the Madison County Health Department

Questions

Madison County Septic FAQs

The questions we get most often from homeowners on their own system.

How often should a Madison County home pump its septic tank?

Every 3–5 years for most households. Larger families, garbage disposals, and heavy laundry days push that toward the shorter end. If you have no idea when yours was last done, that by itself is a good reason to have us out — we will tell you what the sludge level says about your schedule.

I just bought a place in the county and have no idea where the tank is.

That is one of the most common calls we get out here. We locate the tank, uncover the lid, and pump it. While we are there we can set risers so the lid comes to grade — the next service call is then a five-minute job instead of an afternoon with a shovel.

Who permits a new or replacement septic system in Madison County?

Onsite sewage systems in Alabama are permitted through the county health department — here, the Madison County Health Department. When we install or replace a system, we coordinate the permits and inspections as part of the job.

My yard stays wet over the drain field. Is that the septic?

Usually, yes. Standing water, spongy ground, or grass that is suspiciously green over the field lines generally means effluent is surfacing instead of soaking in. Sometimes the fix is a pump-out and easing the load; sometimes the field needs repair or replacement. We look before we quote.

Do you cover the far corners of the county?

We do — Maysville to Triana, Hazel Green down to New Hope, and everything between. We also cover Morgan and Limestone Counties, plus Lawrence County and parts of southern Tennessee.
